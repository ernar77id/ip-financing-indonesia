# Lend against the cash, not the copyright

*Since 2023 any Indonesian lender may take intellectual property as security. On its own it changes almost nothing in a lender's numbers. The money is in the receivables the IP produces, and in two things the market still lacks: loss cover and a buyer.*

*The **Note** in section 4 comes from programme material, not public sources.*

## 1. Summary

- **The law says yes.** Any bank or non-bank, P2P included, may take IP as security (PP 24/2022, POJK 19/2025); KUR has accepted it as additional collateral since January 2026.
- **The balance sheet says no.** Neither commercial banks nor multifinance companies may deduct IP from their provisioning minimum, and a rural bank's 20% allowance for other collateral is unconfirmed for IP. No lender can yet show the recoveries an expected-loss model needs. There is no buyer for repossessed IP and no IP-specific loss cover.
- **The cash says maybe.** The same regulation accepts receivables. Royalties, licence fees and network payments are owed by companies, and invoice-finance lenders already know how to lend against them. A multifinance company that lends by factoring, buying a creative business's receivables under a notarial deed, can already deduct them from its provisioning minimum; a loan merely secured by assigned royalties cannot.
- **The market is narrow and top-heavy.** The collecting body paid Rp151.8 billion to 16,332 music rights holders for most of 2025's use, an average of Rp9.3 million. Borrowers who can carry a loan on IP cash probably number in the hundreds or low thousands.
- **The fix is a stack:** receivables first, credit insurance for up to 75%, and a standby buyer with a price floor. All three start from one number, what the cash is worth; section 10 describes a calculator that measures it.
- **The rate decides who moves first.** At 5 to 10 defaults in 100, a bank needs 13 to 21% to break even, above the 10.5 to 12.5% it charges small businesses. A multifinance company needs 20 to 29%, inside the 18 to 30% it already charges on productive loans; a P2P lender needs 21 to 30%, inside its 36.5% cap. Borrowers inside KUR's caps are better served by its subsidised rate, from 6%.

| P2P lending outstanding / productive share | Rp106.94 trillion / about 33% | Aug / Jun 2026 |

Three readings.

**The sector already borrows, mostly without IP.** KUR is the only published lending series for the creative economy (Rp5.85 trillion in H1 2026; the ministries' own figures disagree), and none of it counts loans secured by IP.

**The IP cash is concentrated.** The average music rights holder received Rp9.3 million from the collecting system for six to nine months of 2025, while the top recipient at one collecting society (WAMI) received Rp730.8 million in its March 2025 distribution. Distribution lags collection by six to twelve months, and Rp65.5 billion of royalties from earlier periods sat unclaimed at the end of 2025 (2025 accounts). The addressable borrowers are the top of each pyramid, and creative companies (labels, publishers, studios, creator networks) more than individual creators.

**P2P lenders need productive loans.** Productive lending was about a third of the P2P book in June 2026 (33%), against OJK's target of 40-50% for 2025-2026. Loans to creative businesses are productive, so they help platforms close that gap.

## 3. What the law allows

| Instrument | What it does |
|---|---|
| PP 24/2022, in force 12 Jul 2023 | IP-based financing from any bank or licensed non-bank. Three forms of security: fiducia over the IP, contracts, and receivables in creative-economy activity (Art 9(2)) |
| Copyright Law 28/2014, Art 16(3) | Copyright may be the object of fiducia |
| POJK 19/2025, in force 2 Nov 2025 | Every bank and non-bank, P2P included, may accept IP as security in small-business financing (Arts 2, 13(2)) |
| Permenko 1/2026, in force 13 Jan 2026 | IP named as allowed additional KUR collateral (Art 19(3)); no extra collateral may be required up to Rp100 million (Art 20(1)) |
| Perpres 37/2026 | Creative economy master plan; a state IP financing vehicle, the Indonesia Creative Content Fund (ICCF), planned by 2029 |

- **Which loans.** Any loan to an Indonesian creative-economy business, in any amount (PP 24/2022 Arts 1(2), 1(4)). The Rp100 million line applies only to KUR.
- **What IP qualifies.** IP recorded or registered with the IP office, DJKI, and already exploited (Art 10).

| Date | Step |

**Note.** A pilot inside KUR is testing the full chain with five creative businesses: valuation by a registered IP valuer, credit analysis by the bank, then IP taken as collateral. A second phase started in August 2026. It is meant to produce an internal guideline for banks on IP as collateral, and technical guidance on applying SPI 321, the Indonesian valuation standard for IP used as security for debt. Every step is about valuing IP; none is about the contracts and receivables that PP 24/2022 lists in the same article.

Nobody publishes how many loans were actually secured by IP. In July 2026 a deputy chair of parliament's Commission VII said banks still do not fully accept IP certificates, and that only nine valuation firms can value IP (the 64 registered valuers work inside such firms).

## 5. Why IP alone does not carry a loan

| Lender | How a bad loan is provided for | Does IP collateral help? |
|---|---|---|
| Commercial bank | Provisions on the balance minus eligible collateral, from a closed list (POJK 40/2019 Art 45) | No. IP is not on the list, and no amendment was located |
| Rural bank (BPR) | May count 20% of other collateral valued by an independent valuer in the last year (POJK 1/2024 Art 20(1)(k)) | Possibly; OJK has not confirmed IP qualifies |
| Multifinance | Minimum allowance of 1/5/15/50/100% by quality on the balance net of collateral (POJK 35/2018 Art 97, unamended). Deductible collateral is a closed list: cash, securities, vehicles, equipment, inventory, warehouse receipts, machinery, ships and aircraft, property, plus receivables bought as factoring and leased assets (SEOJK 1/SEOJK.05/2016 Part IV). The accounting allowance under PSAK 109 applies where it is higher. Net non-performing above 5%, up to 25%, triggers intensive supervision (POJK 49/2024 Art 24) | No for IP: it is not on the list. Yes for receivables it buys as factoring under a notarial deed, not for receivables taken only as security; untested for an individual creator's royalties. The accounting allowance falls only on evidenced recoveries |
| P2P lender | The platform holds no loans; funders bear the loss, and banks provide about two-thirds of the funding (64% of outstanding, Nov 2025). Platforms must keep 90-day arrears under 5% (industry 4.73%, Aug 2026) | Depends on the funder's own rules |

Four problems apply to every lender:

- **Recoveries need evidence.** Under the accounting standard PSAK 109 (formerly PSAK 71), collateral lowers expected loss only by the recovery a lender can expect, net of the costs of selling. With no sales history, the IP recovery rounds to zero.
- **No buyer.** No Indonesian music catalogue or film library sale was disclosed from 2020 to 2026; the last visible one is Warner's purchase of an Indonesian label catalogue in 2016. Auction rules list IP among the intangibles the state auction office can sell (PMK 122/2023 Art 6(2)), but no IP auction has been found, and a law firm reports the office does not yet accept IP in practice.
- **No loss cover.** Unlike Malaysia, Korea and China, Indonesia has no IP-specific loss-sharing, guarantee or insurance scheme. Singapore ran one from 2014 to 2018 and retired it after lending to three companies, citing the valuation cost and the missing buyer: the same two gaps as here. What ICCF will do is not written down.
- **File cost on small tickets.** At legal caps, the notary, registration and recording fees on a Rp50 million loan come to about Rp1.75 million (3.5% of principal), and about Rp4.4 million (1.75%) on Rp250 million, before the valuation; about Rp4.2 million for a song, whose recording has been free since 1 August 2026. No valuer publishes a fee, though PP 24/2022 lets a lender value through a panel it appoints (Art 12).

## 6. Who pays the borrower

| Receivable | Who pays | Evidence | Can it be assigned? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Music performing royalties | LMKN, through the creator's collecting society (LMK), twice a year, six to twelve months or more after use | LMK statements | No rule either way; the LMK reports to LMKN who was paid (Permenkum 27/2025 Art 28(3)(b)). Needs the LMK's written acknowledgement and payment direction; untested, so take legal advice |
| Streaming royalties | The distributor or label | Distributor statements, public stream counts | Not without the distributor's written consent: DistroKid, TuneCore, CD Baby and ONErpm all bar assignment |
| Film and series licences | Streaming services, broadcasters | Licence agreements | Yes, by written assignment with notice to the payer (Civil Code Art 613), unless the contract bars it; check each contract |
| Brand deals and trademark licences | Brands, licensees | Signed contracts; licence recorded at DJKI | Yes, by written assignment with notice, unless the contract bars it; check each contract |
| Creator network (MCN) payments | The MCN, which is YouTube's payee for its affiliated channels | MCN statements, public channel data | Yes, with the MCN's acknowledgement, if its creator contract allows it; no MCN publishes its contract, so read each one |
| AdSense on a creator's own channel | Google Asia Pacific | YouTube Studio and AdSense exports | No: Google's terms bar it (see below) |

Most of these are invoices from companies. A lender that factors trade receivables already has the process: verify the contract, notify the payer, collect into an account the payer pays.

**Platform income is the exception.** Google's AdSense terms for Indonesia bar a creator from assigning payouts or letting a third party receive them without Google's written authorisation, and an account terminated for breach loses all further payment. A lender can watch AdSense income arrive, not take it. A creator paid through an MCN is different: the MCN is YouTube's payee, so the receivable is owed by a company.

Streaming income on the recording side goes through the label or distributor, not the collecting system, wherever the listener is. About 60% of Indonesian artists' Spotify royalties in 2025 came from abroad, so the distributor's statement is also the evidence of foreign income. DistroKid and TuneCore now advance royalties themselves and are repaid first, so check for an existing advance before lending.

**Many authors, one product.** A song can have several composers and lyricists, a publisher, performers and a producer, and no rule, and no court decision found, says whether fiducia can be taken over one owner's share of a co-owned copyright. Lend against the borrower's own share of the receivable, checked against DJKI's public song and music data centre (PDLM) and the borrower's LMK statements. Only about 26,000 songs are in the data centre, which LMKN is meant to distribute from (PP 56/2021); the 2025 ministerial rule has each collecting society distribute on its own calculation of usage (Permenkum 27/2025 Art 28(1)), so check both before counting the income. DJKI copyright recording, a separate register, has been free for songs since 1 August 2026.

## 7. Three fixes, one stack

Section 5 found that IP gives no provisioning relief and no evidenced recovery, has no buyer and has no loss cover. Receivables answer the recovery problem for every lender, and the provisioning problem for a multifinance company that lends by factoring; insurance answers loss cover; the standby buyer answers the missing buyer. The file cost stays a cost, priced in section 8.

**Receivables first.** Lend against what the payers in section 6 owe, sized on the borrower's share and on income that lasts without new work. Take fiducia over the copyright or trademark as a lock that stops the asset moving (Fiducia Law Art 23), not as the source of repayment.

**Credit insurance for up to 75%.** Credit insurance and guarantees already exist for banks, multifinance and P2P funders. The lender must keep at least 25% of the balance uninsured (POJK 20/2023 Art 5; the same split for guarantors under POJK 11/2025 Art 37), and OJK has been reviewing the rule since April 2026; in June 2026 it said the rule still applies. Premiums on P2P credit insurance are already high (the P2P association AFPI, September 2026), and an insurer with no loss history for creative loans will price higher still. Insurance bought outside the loan contract may not reduce the expected-loss allowance (IFRS Interpretations Committee, March 2019): ask the auditor before counting it.

**A standby buyer with a price floor.** A third party agrees at origination to buy the pledged IP on default. Fiducia law shapes the deal. Default must be agreed with the borrower or found by a court (Constitutional Court 18/PUU-XVII/2019). The IP is then sold at public auction or, with the borrower's agreement, privately at the highest obtainable price after one month's notice and two newspaper announcements (Fiducia Law Art 29). The lender cannot take the IP itself on default (Art 33), so the buyer must be a third party, and a lender-controlled buyer would be open to the same challenge. A price fixed in advance can be challenged as not the highest obtainable (Arts 29(1)(c), 32). Neither reading has been tested on IP in court; take legal advice on the agreement. So the agreement sets a floor and a duty to market for one month, not a fixed price. Candidates are the major labels, regional catalogue funds and large creator networks. Price evidence is regional: Universal paid $45.5 million in 2023, plus up to $4.9 million contingent, for 70% of a Thai catalogue of more than 10,000 songs (RS Group filing to the Thai stock exchange and press release, 2023).

**How the stack works.** On default the insurer pays up to 75% of the balance and then shares recoveries in proportion. The standby buyer sets a floor under those recoveries. For the first time, someone other than the lender has a reason to price the asset, and all three parties need the same number: what the cash and the IP are worth, and to whom.

## 8. What rate makes it pay

What must one loan earn before the lender stops losing money? Take a 12-month, Rp250 million productive loan repaid in one sum at the end (with monthly instalments, running and file costs per rupiah outstanding roughly double). The rate covers the cost of money, running cost, the loan file and the defaults, which pay no interest, so the surviving loans carry them. With no default data for creative loans, the table assumes two rates.

| % of the loan, one year | Bank | Multifinance | P2P lender |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pays for the money | 3, above the largest banks' blended cost of funds (1.1 to 2.4%) | 10, a mid-tier company's borrowing cost | 12, funders' return after losses |
| Running cost | 4 | 4 | 3, platform fee |
| Loan file, at legal caps | 1.75 | 1.75 | 1.75 |
| **Rate needed with no defaults** | **8.75** | **15.75** | **16.75** |
| 5 in 100 default, nothing recovered | 14.5 | 21.8 | 22.9 |
| 5 in 100 default, buyer pays a floor of 30% of the balance | 12.9 | 20.3 | 21.3 |
| 10 in 100 default, nothing recovered | 20.8 | 28.6 | 29.7 |
| 10 in 100 default, buyer pays a floor of 30% | 17.5 | 25.3 | 26.4 |
| What it usually charges or may charge | 10.5 to 12.5 on small-business loans | 18 to 30 on productive loans; no formal cap | at most 36.5 (0.1% a day on the original principal, platform fee included; SEOJK 19/SEOJK.06/2025) |

*The cost of money, running cost, default rates and buyer's floor are the author's assumptions, set against the market data in the sources. The loan file cost and the P2P cap are legal figures; the last row is market pricing.*

How to read it. Rate needed = (costs + defaults times share lost) / share that survives; with 5 defaults in 100 and nothing recovered, a bank needs (8.75 + 5) / 0.95 = 14.5. The rows assume defaults come before any interest and that the income itself has failed; receivables that keep paying recover more. So these are conservative break-even rates, before any return on capital.

The reading. A bank needs 13 to 21% against the 10.5 to 12.5% it charges small businesses, so it waits for evidenced recoveries; a smaller bank paying market deposit rates needs about 3 points more. A multifinance company needs 20 to 29%, inside the 18 to 30% it already charges, and, if it lends by factoring, can count the purchased receivables against its provisioning minimum (section 5): it is the natural first lender. A P2P lender needs 21 to 30%, inside its cap; at a typical 23% all-in (Modalku's published example, 18% plus a 5% fee) it covers 5 defaults in 100 and loses money at 10 unless it prices near the cap. The buyer's floor cuts every lender's number by 1.6 to 3.3 points.

Multifinance has no formal rate cap, though OJK's 2024-2028 roadmap plans one, POJK 46/2024 Art 19J lets it cap digital loans, and every lender must keep a pricing guideline (POJK 22/2023 Art 28).

Why would a borrower not take KUR at 6%? Borrowers inside KUR's caps are better served there. IP-based KUR runs through the state banks, for loans of Rp100-500 million to businesses with turnover up to Rp4.8 billion a year. The case here is for creative companies above those caps, larger tickets, and borrowers that P2P and multifinance lenders serve.

Insurance does not lower the rate. The insurer prices the 75% it covers off the same defaults: at 10 in 100 that is at least 7.5% of the loan before its margin. What insurance buys is a known cost in place of an uncertain loss, which matters for capital and for how much funders will commit. The floor and the premium both depend on loss and recovery data that does not yet exist. That data starts with the first loans booked on a borrowing base the parties share; the calculator in section 10 records each loan, default and recovery in one format that insurers and buyers can read.

## 9. Risks and responses

| Risk | Early sign | Response |
|---|---|---|
| Insurers will not price it | Quotes above the margin; the 75/25 rule unchanged | Insure a pool, not single loans; ask the network, label or publisher whose creators borrow to take a first-loss slice; give insurers the loss and recovery record of every loan booked, in the calculator's format, every quarter |
| No standby buyer signs | Majors and regional funds decline a floor | Sell the receivable stream instead of the IP; start with buyers who already earn from the catalogue; start with music, where regional price evidence exists |
| The receivables are too small | Lasting income below the file cost outside the top tier | Lend to creative companies rather than individuals; group small receivables under one payer into one file |
| Payers will not acknowledge | Payment directions refused, assignment barred | Start with payers that gain when their own creators get funding, such as networks and distributors |
| An existing advance outranks the lender | A distributor advance on the statement (DistroKid, TuneCore), recouped from the same royalties | Net the unrecouped advance off the borrowing base, or lend only after recoupment |
| The platforms close the door | Derived-metrics amendment refused; lending on creator exports treated as a breach | Shift to receivables owed by companies (networks, distributors, brands); ask Google for the written authorisation its terms allow |
| Platform policy cuts income | Partner Program rules tighten from 1 Feb 2027; Shorts revenue needs 10 million views per 90 days; channel-level enforcement removed large AI channels in Jan 2026, two Indonesian | Record the format mix, Shorts share of revenue and policy-strike history; cut Shorts and new-upload income hard; make loss of Partner Program status a loan trigger |
| Ownership expires | Songs sold outright revert to the author after 25 years, plus two years for sales signed before the 2014 law (Copyright Law Arts 18, 122), so 1999 sales return in 2026; the transition rule is untested in court | Check the date of every assignment; size the loan to the years the borrower still owns the right |
| The wrong asset is taken | The account itself (not IP that DJKI records, so outside PP 24/2022 Art 10 in the author's reading; access without right is a crime, Criminal Code Art 332), or a work made only by AI (not protected, per DJKI, November 2025) | Take the receivable, not the account; check authorship and AI use before relying on a copyright |
| The state solves it first | ICCF takes first loss, or IP joins the provisioning list | Banks then lend against IP itself, and still need a price: the calculator serves ICCF-covered loans the same way |
| Valuers cannot rely on the calculator | A valuer refuses to sign on calculator output; SPI 321 or the valuers' code may limit reliance on a third party's model; the standard is sold, not published, so check it with the valuer | Deliver the calculator's output as the valuer's working paper, every assumption visible; the valuer signs |

## 10. The prototype: a creator income calculator

Every layer of the stack needs the same first number: how much cash the borrower earns, from whom, and how much of it lasts. The prototype measures it in one tool with two steps. Step 1, an estimator, sizes creator income from the accounts that produce it. Step 2, a loan view, starts from that estimate and shows which of the income a lender can count under its own settings.

**Step 1: the income estimator.** Ad and sponsorship income start with content and audience. The estimator reads them from links where a platform allows, and from what the creator connects or uploads where it does not.

- **In:** links to the creator's public YouTube and Instagram accounts. TikTok needs the creator's own connection.
- **YouTube, through the official API:** followers, median views, engagement, upload rhythm, growth, category, format mix and declared sponsored posts. Turning them into estimates needs YouTube's derived-metrics amendment, which is open only to audited analytics developers. The beta reads public statistics with an API key before any amendment, so it runs ahead of that policy until Google accepts the use in writing.
- **YouTube, optionally through a connection the creator authorises:** watch time, audience country, traffic sources and the Shorts share of views, through the non-monetary Analytics scope only and revocable by the creator; the revenue scope is never requested.
- **Instagram, by link for business and creator accounts:** followers, posting rhythm, Reel views and interactions, through Meta's Business Discovery API. They price a sponsored post. Meta bars its API data from eligibility decisions, credit included (Platform Terms 3.a.ii), so the numbers a lender decides on come from the creator's own export: views, viewers and interactions for the last 90 days or more, downloaded from Meta Business Suite on desktop. In the author's reading an owner's own download is not API data; take legal advice before relying on it.
- **TikTok, not in the beta:** TikTok's API returns follower, like and video counts only with the account owner's consent; there is no route by link alone. A later version adds it through a connection the creator authorises.
- **Applies market rates:** ad rates for the audience (median CPM for Indonesia about $0.87, with 55% of long-form ad revenue going to the creator) and sponsorship rates by category and audience size, from rates the user sets or the creator's own deal records.
- **Out:** a monthly income range by source (ads, sponsorship, affiliate), a price range per sponsored post by format, the share likely to last without new uploads, and a confidence level. It values the income, not the account: the account itself is not pledgeable IP (section 9).

The ranges are wide on public data. A channel with 2 million long-form views a month shows about Rp4.7 to 13.6 million a month from ads, because published Indonesian rates differ about threefold. A connected YouTube account replaces the national average with the channel's own audience mix; only uploaded YouTube Studio, AdSense and sponsorship records narrow the range to what was actually paid.

**Step 2: the loan view.** It takes the estimate and the statements a borrower uploads, or sample data, and asks four questions of every income stream.

| Question | What it checks |
|---|---|
| How much? | Size and trend, from statements, exports and contracts |
| How long without new work? | Back-catalogue and evergreen income against income that stops when uploads stop |
| Who pays? | The payer: collecting society, distributor, MCN, brand or platform |
| Can the lender control it? | Assignable, payment can be directed, or visible only, as with AdSense |

- **In:** bank and e-wallet statements; LMK, distributor, MCN and platform statements or exports; contracts and licences; DJKI and PDLM records; the estimator's output.
- **Out:**
  - income by payer, scored on the four questions, and the gap between what the borrower reports and what reached the bank account
  - the borrower's share where a work has several owners, and payer concentration
  - a borrowing base under the user's own settings: rate, tenor, fees, haircut
  - whether the receivables count as deductible collateral under the lender's rules, such as receivables bought as factoring by a multifinance company (section 5)
  - recovery value with comparables, and the effect of a buyer's floor
  - stress cases: a platform policy change, a payer delay, the royalty lag of six to twelve months
  - the break-even rate for the user's lender type, by the method in section 8

**The data rules it works within.**

- **No revenue sign-in data.** Google bars data from its sensitive and restricted scopes "to determine credit-worthiness or for lending purposes". The YouTube revenue scope is sensitive, so the calculator never requests it; the non-monetary Analytics scope showed as non-sensitive in the author's Google Cloud Console test project (19 Oct 2026); Google confirms the class at app verification, and YouTube's developer policies apply either way.
- **Public data under amendment.** YouTube's derived-metrics policy is silent on lending, so the use must be disclosed and accepted in writing. TikTok's terms bar building profiles of individuals.

**What it never does.** It does not sign a valuation (a licensed valuer does), make a credit decision, hold money, scrape, or hold a password. The beta reads public data by link; later versions add what the creator connects or uploads. It reports facts and applies the user's own parameters. A third party supplying a credit-assessment method must be licensed under POJK 19/2025 Art 14(3). It is built to report and calculate, not to supply a method, but OJK has not ruled on tools like it; a lender should take legal advice before relying on it.

**The beta.** [resolva.pro/calculator](https://www.resolva.pro/calculator/) runs step 1 for YouTube and Instagram by link, plus a small loan view from step 2. It shows a monthly income range by source, a price per sponsored post by format, the share of income that lasts without new uploads, and a borrowing base under the user's own rate, tenor and haircut, next to the section 8 break-even rate for the user's lender type. Statements, payers and the four questions come in a later version.

- **Prices:** expected views x a cost per 1,000 views for Indonesia x format and niche multipliers, never below a floor fee. Views above 500,000 per post count at half, because large channels sell reach cheaper. Published rate cards are shown beside the estimate as a ceiling, not as expected income.
- **Design choice: plain arithmetic, no AI model.** The same accounts and settings always give the same numbers, and every default is on screen and editable. A lender, an auditor and a valuer can each trace a figure to its rate; a model would hide the step they need to check.
- **Data:** public account data only, held in server memory for six hours and never written to disk. No sign-in, no cookies.

- Works made entirely by AI without human creative contribution are not protected: DJKI, "Karya Berbasis Kecerdasan Buatan, Milik Siapa?", 17 Nov 2025. [DJKI](https://dgip.go.id/artikel/detail-artikel-berita/karya-berbasis-kecerdasan-buatan-milik-siapa?kategori=liputan-humas)
